# 2026 Week 3 — Single-Entry Post-Slate Review

## Lineup

The Week 3 $50 single-entry lineup was:

| Position | Player |
|---|---|
| QB | Tyler Shough |
| RB | Kenneth Walker III |
| RB | Jaylen Warren |
| WR | Amon-Ra St. Brown |
| WR | Jalen Coker |
| WR | Devaughn Vele |
| TE | Dalton Schultz |
| FLEX | Breece Hall |
| DST | Vikings DST |

Total salary: **$50,000**

This review focuses on the construction process rather than simply labeling the lineup a success or failure.

## What the lineup was trying to do

The lineup combined several individually attractive projected values with a few lower-owned decisions.

The construction leaned on:

- salary efficiency at quarterback,
- strong projected individual plays,
- late-news opportunity,
- Breece Hall as a leverage piece,
- value at wide receiver and tight end to make the salary structure work.

The problem was that those ideas were evaluated too independently.

A tournament lineup is not just nine isolated player projections. The players need to create a realistic path to a top-end lineup score together.

## Tyler Shough — salary efficiency was weighted too heavily

The quarterback decision emphasized salary savings and point-per-dollar projection.

That can be useful in cash games or as one part of a tournament decision, but quarterback is also one of the easiest places to build correlation and access a high-scoring game environment.

The Week 3 construction saved salary at quarterback without creating enough additional ceiling elsewhere.

### Process change

Quarterback selection should begin with:

1. Which games have realistic 30+ point team outcomes?
2. Which quarterbacks can produce a tournament-level score?
3. Which pass catchers create useful correlation?
4. Only then: how does salary affect the rest of the lineup?

Salary efficiency remains an input, not the starting point.

## Lineup correlation was too weak

The lineup contained several reasonable individual plays but did not tell a strong game-level story.

A quarterback stack can benefit from the same scoring events:

```text
QB throws touchdown
    ->
QB scores
    +
receiver scores
```

An opposing bring-back can also benefit when a competitive game forces both teams to continue playing aggressively.

Week 3 did not use enough of this relationship.

### Process change

For single-entry tournaments, prioritize at least one clear correlated scoring environment unless there is a specific reason not to.

Correlation should not be added mechanically. The players still need strong roles and ceiling.

## Jalen Coker — late news was converted into too much certainty

Late news improved Coker's opportunity.

The error was treating "more opportunity" as if it automatically meant "large target share."

Those are different statements.

A player's competition for snaps or routes can decrease without the offense necessarily concentrating enough targets on that player for a tournament ceiling.

### Process change

Late news should update variables such as:

- expected snaps,
- routes,
- carries,
- target competition,
- red-zone opportunity.

It should not automatically convert a value play into a core tournament play.

## Breece Hall — leverage was forced

The Breece Hall decision was partly justified by ownership.

Lower ownership can be useful because a strong result gains more ground on the field.

But ownership alone does not create upside.

The more important question is:

> If this player is lower owned, what realistic football outcome gives him a score that can win the tournament?

The Week 3 decision leaned too much on being different and not enough on whether Hall's ceiling case was strong enough relative to the alternatives.

### Process change

Use leverage only when both are true:

1. the player is meaningfully different from the field,
2. the player has a believable tournament-winning ceiling.

For single entry, one or two strong leverage decisions are enough. The lineup does not need to be different everywhere.

## Devaughn Vele and Dalton Schultz — useful salary, limited ceiling

Both players helped the lineup fit under the salary cap.

The issue was the cumulative effect.

One lower-ceiling salary-saving player can be acceptable if the savings unlock major upside elsewhere. Multiple salary-driven plays can leave the lineup with too few realistic paths to a first-place-type score.

### Process change

Every non-DST roster spot should answer:

> What is this player's realistic path to materially exceeding salary-based expectations?

If that answer is weak, the salary savings need to produce substantial ceiling somewhere else in the lineup.

## What was handled better

The review did not find every decision flawed.

Kenneth Walker III, Jaylen Warren, and Amon-Ra St. Brown were stronger parts of the original process.

The important lesson is not to abandon good projected plays simply because a lineup lost.

The goal is to identify which decisions were supported by good process and which parts of the construction weakened the lineup as a whole.

## The Lawrence/Parker lesson

A Lawrence/Parker construction had been identified before lock as a stronger game-environment thesis.

That matters because it means the better idea was available before results were known.

The post-slate lesson is therefore not hindsight such as:

> "I should have played whoever scored the most."

It is:

> "I identified a more correlated, higher-ceiling game thesis before lock but did not allow that thesis to drive the final construction."

That is a repeatable process lesson.

## Revised construction order

The Week 3 review changed the order of operations to:

```text
game environment
    ->
correlated ceiling
    ->
player projections
    ->
ownership
    ->
1-2 deliberate leverage decisions
    ->
final lineup
```

### 1. Game environment

Start with games capable of producing strong fantasy scoring.

### 2. Correlated ceiling

Build combinations that can score together rather than treating every position independently.

### 3. Projections

Use projections to compare the expected strength of the remaining player pool.

### 4. Ownership

Determine how popular those plays are likely to be.

### 5. Leverage

Use a small number of lower-owned decisions when the ceiling case supports them.

### 6. Final lineup review

Before entering, ask:

- Does this lineup have enough ceiling?
- Where does the score come from if the lineup wins?
- Which players succeed together?
- Am I forcing any play only because it is cheap?
- Am I forcing any play only because it is low owned?
- Did late news improve the underlying role, or am I assuming too much?

## Week 3 takeaway

The main issue was not that every player projection was wrong.

The construction over-weighted **salary efficiency and isolated player value** while under-weighting **game environment, correlation, and total lineup ceiling**.

That distinction is the foundation for the process used in future weeks.
